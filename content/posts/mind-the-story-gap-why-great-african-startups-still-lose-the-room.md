## The Pitch That Missed

*Sandbox Réseau meetup, builders' demo.*

The landing page was lovely. It had real metrics, a tight structure and a founder who knew the numbers by heart. When he finished, the room went quiet. It wasn't a thoughtful silence. It was the silence of 20+ founders, builders and potential customers who weren't sure what they had just heard.

As the convener of Sandbox Réseau, I've seen that silence more times than I can count. The founders aren't short of substance. Many of them have impressive landing pages, genuine traction and polished delivery. Still, their pitches fall flat because there's a gap between what they're offering and what the room understands, believes or feels about it.

That observation made me curious about storytelling and the role it plays in business. I went looking for answers in a range of resources and eventually found *Stories That Stick* by Kindra Hall.



## The Story Gap Is Wider in Emerging Markets

Hall describes a "story gap" in every business: the disconnect between product and customer, founder and investor, mission and team. In a market that runs on trust, closing that gap isn't a nice-to-have. It's fundamental.

Hall's framework gives founders four types of stories and a repeatable structure for telling them. I decided to test it on one of my own products, [Minutes2Match](https://minutes2match.com). My hope is to find a system that works and to share what I learn along the way. Do come along with me.

In many African markets, the gap is structurally wider, for three reasons.

1. **Investors lack context.** Many of the investors with capital to deploy in Africa are based elsewhere and haven't lived the problems African founders are solving. They often don't know the market rails a business is built on, such as mobile money, informal trade and WhatsApp commerce. Your story has to carry all of that context.
2. **Customers lack trust.** Trust is low in commercial environments, especially where transactions happen face to face or over WhatsApp. A simple landing page won't win anyone over. People need to know who you are, why they should believe you and who else already trusts you.
3. **Attention is limited.** When your audience is unfamiliar with your market, every minute of their attention counts, and stories compress context. A well-crafted narrative can convey in 60 seconds what five slides of market-sizing data can't.



## The Four Stories Every African Startup Needs

### 1. The Value Story

> The value story bridges the gap between what you offer and what the customer needs. It doesn't describe features. It shows transformation.

In my experience, this is the story founders struggle with most, and I was no exception. Minutes2Match started out as speed dating events here in Accra. At the time, our value story amounted to this: *"Oh, it's a speed dating event where you have about five to seven minutes to talk to someone and see if there's a spark."* The next question, as you can imagine, was usually *"What's speed dating, and how does it work?"* We had communicated almost nothing, and it was fair of people to ask.

I've visited a lot of startup landing pages just to get a sense of what they do, and I'm often left thinking, *I still don't know what this is.* That's a potential customer lost.

Today, Minutes2Match goes beyond events. We get to know our members and introduce them to a curated match every week. Here's how we tell it now:

> Raj is a young accountant at a fast-paced financial institution. Between long days and early mornings, he barely has time to see friends, let alone meet someone new. After a recent breakup, he decides he's ready to start dating again. He downloads a few dating apps, but the swipe culture wears him down. The conversations go nowhere, and the people he meets aren't who he's looking for. Before long, he's demotivated and unenthusiastic about putting himself out there at all.
>
> Then a close friend tells him about Minutes2Match. He signs up, tells us exactly what he's looking for, and we take the time to get to know him. He opts in to be matched once a week. Three weeks in, he's introduced to someone he actually wants to see again, and then he does. For the first time in months, Raj is hopeful.

That isn't a pitch. It's a story, and it answers every question a pitch should: who the product is for, the pain it eases, why it works and how it makes life better.

**Where to use it:** customer conversations, landing pages, investor decks (the "why this matters" moment) and demo days.

### 2. The Founder Story

> The founder story explains the origin of the company and the "why" behind it. It humanises the brand and builds trust.

The founder story is how investors, customers, new hires and partners are won over. Investors in particular care about how personal the problem is to the founder. They don't just bet on the product. They're far more likely to back a founder who has experienced the problem first-hand, understood why existing solutions failed and built the answer because nobody else would.

I recently added an [About Us page](https://minutes2match.com/about) to the Minutes2Match website. We didn't have one before, and the brand lacked any sense of the people behind it. We came across as a mysterious group of people hell-bent on matching strangers and running speed dating events. So I put a face to the brand. I told the story of how it started and why, what we learned that pushed us in a new direction, and what we're promising and planning next. Take a look and let me know what you think.

*PS: This isn't an invitation to write your autobiography. Your founder story has to be relevant and tied to the reason the business exists.*

**Where to use it:** investor meetings, podcast interviews, About pages, and first conversations with potential partners and hires.

### 3. The Purpose Story

> The purpose story aligns your team around a mission. It bridges the gap between the work and the meaning behind it.

For a startup, or any business for that matter, you'll spend nearly every waking moment working to win customers and users. That makes it essential to keep a strong team that is aligned and understands the mission. People are motivated when the impact of their work is something they can internalise and care about. Nothing communicates that better than a purpose story. It has to go beyond a table tennis table and equity that may never vest.

It's tempting to think a small team like ours doesn't need this. I'd argue the opposite. In a small team every person carries more weight, so every person needs to believe in the mission.

The purpose story is also the one that turns users into evangelists. In markets where word of mouth is the main distribution channel, a customer who can retell your purpose story to a friend is worth more than a paid ad.

Here's ours:

> We're more connected online than any generation before us, and somehow more disconnected in real life. Many of us have stood at that point where we believed life could be better, and that we could share it with someone we actually like. Minutes2Match exists for that moment. Our mission is to bring intentionality back to dating and to spark meaningful introductions at a pace that feels human, one week at a time.

If I shared that with a new hire, I'd hope she'd be asking when she can start. That's the job of the purpose story: to give your team a mission worth their while and one they're willing to throw their weight behind.

**Where to use it:** hiring conversations, team all-hands, onboarding and community building.

### 4. The Customer Story

> The customer story uses real experiences as social proof. It lets prospects see themselves in someone else's success.

In markets that run on trust and referrals, the customer story isn't optional. It's the main way you sell. People buy because someone they know bought, and the customer story formalises what's already happening informally.

With Minutes2Match, we learned early how much depends on customer feedback, and in this part of the world, word of mouth is on another level. When we were running physical events, we spent heavily on sponsored ads on Meta. We got the attention we wanted, but we hit a problem. The events were private, so we couldn't use photos or videos from them in our promotion. Understandably, people looking to meet someone didn't want to be tagged doing it. Meanwhile, people who hadn't attended kept asking what an event looked and felt like.

So we collected written feedback from attendees. With their permission, we turned it into anonymised stories like this one:

> Basiwa had had enough of swiping fatigue and of watching dating apps turn into hookup apps. One Saturday afternoon she came across a Minutes2Match ad on Instagram. She was sceptical, but she made a mental note to give it a try. When she finally came to one of our events, she spoke to more new people in a single evening than she had in the previous six months. In one of our regular check-ins, she told us she'd started talking to someone whose company she genuinely enjoys, and she's hopeful about where it leads.

Most of our marketing up to that point had been generic. This captured a full customer journey while protecting her privacy.

A common mistake is waiting until you have "big" customers before telling customer stories. The most relatable ones often come from early users with modest beginnings and a clear before and after.

**Where to use it:** sales conversations, landing pages, investor updates and social proof on social media.



## The Structure That Makes Stories Land

Hall sets out a pattern for telling compelling stories. If you didn't notice it in the Minutes2Match stories above, go back and look. They all follow the same three-part structure:

**Normal → Explosion → New Normal**

- **Normal:** Introduce a character the audience can identify with, and describe their world before the change. Make it specific and sensory, not abstract.
- **Explosion:** The moment something shifts. The problem becomes unbearable, the product enters the picture or the insight lands. This tension is what holds attention.
- **New Normal:** The transformed state. What does life look like now? Be concrete. Numbers help, but only once the emotional arc is in place.

This structure works especially well here. Many African cultures have deep storytelling traditions, and the Normal → Explosion → New Normal arc isn't foreign to them. It mirrors how stories are naturally told in conversation, at market stalls and in community gatherings. Founders who use it aren't importing a Western framework. They're drawing on something that's already ours.

> **A little homework:** take any investor slide, product description or About page and run it through the Normal → Explosion → New Normal filter. If it doesn't have a character, a tension and a transformation, it isn't a story yet. Do this often enough, and it becomes a lens you look through without thinking.




## Find, Craft, Tell — The Execution Layer

> *"Maxwell, I don't think I have any stories to tell. And even if I do, none of them fit. Do I have to make things up?"*

A founder said this to me in a recent conversation, and she sounded helpless. I suspect most founders feel the same way. The good news is that Hall offers a simple three-step process.

1. **Find.** Your stories already exist. They're in customer WhatsApp messages, support tickets and the anecdotes you tell at meetups. The problem isn't a shortage of stories. It's that nobody is capturing them. Start a story bank: a simple doc or Notion page. Every time a customer says something specific about how your product changed things for them, write it down.
2. **Craft.** Raw material isn't a finished story. Apply the Normal → Explosion → New Normal structure. Cut the generic, add the specific, name the character and quantify the transformation where you can.
3. **Tell.** Match the story to the gap.
    - Pitching an investor? Lead with the **founder story** or the **value story**.
    - Hiring? Lead with the **purpose story**.
    - Selling? Lead with the **customer story**.

   Every company needs all four, but each one has a specific job.

And no, you don't have to make anything up. You just have to start paying attention.




## The Competitive Advantage Nobody's Using

Most startups in the African ecosystem compete on product, price or distribution. Very few compete on narrative. That's a gap, and it's one we can close.

The frameworks in *Stories That Stick* aren't abstract storytelling theory. They're operational tools. A founder who can tell a sharp value story in 90 seconds has a real advantage over one who opens with a TAM (total addressable market) chart. Our markets run on trust, our investors are often far away, and word of mouth is how most products spread. The strongest signal a founder can send is how close they are to the problem. In that environment, storytelling isn't a soft skill. It's infrastructure.

So the next time you open your pitch deck, ask yourself: does this have a character, a tension and a transformation?

**If not, you have a presentation. You don't have a story yet.**
